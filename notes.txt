Based on your project scope, your outputs will be defined across two dimensions: **Spatial** (Where) and **Temporal** (When).

### 1. The Spatial Output (Where)

The raw IMD dataset provides rainfall at a 0.25° × 0.25° grid resolution (thousands of points across India). Predicting for individual grid points using classical time-series methods is noisy and computationally expensive.

Instead, you will use `geopandas` and `regionmask` to aggregate these grid points into **IMD's 36 Meteorological Subdivisions** (e.g., "Kerala", "Coastal Karnataka", "Vidarbha").

* **The Output:** A single numerical rainfall value for a specific subdivision. (You will likely train a separate time-series model for each subdivision, or one global model with subdivision as a categorical feature).

### 2. The Temporal Output (When)

You mentioned both seasonal and sub-seasonal forecasting.

* **Sub-seasonal Output (2-week ahead):** The cumulative rainfall (in millimeters) for the upcoming 14-day window. If the current date is July 1, the model outputs the total expected rainfall from July 2 through July 15.
* **Seasonal Output:** The total cumulative rainfall (in millimeters) for the entire core Southwest Monsoon period (June through September, known as JJAS), or the percentage departure from the Long Period Average (LPA).

### 3. What the FastAPI will eventually yield

When you deploy this later, your model will be designed so that your FastAPI endpoint can receive a payload like `{"subdivision": "Kerala", "forecast_horizon": "14-day"}` and return:

1. **Point Forecast:** e.g., `145.2 mm`
2. **Confidence Intervals:** e.g., `[110.5 mm, 180.0 mm]` (Classical models like SARIMAX and Exponential Smoothing are excellent at providing mathematical confidence bounds, which are highly valuable for weather APIs).
